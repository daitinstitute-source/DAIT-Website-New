Data analyst is one of the few roles that a non-engineer can enter within a year and grow in for a decade. It is also one of the most misunderstood — most people assume it means heavy coding or a maths degree. It doesn't. It means asking the right question of a dataset and explaining the answer so a business can act on it.

Here's an honest, step-by-step guide to getting there in 2026, whether you're a fresh graduate or a working professional planning a switch.

## What a data analyst actually does

Strip away the job-ad language and the work is four repeating steps:

- **Get the data** — usually by writing SQL against a company database
- **Clean it** — fix duplicates, missing values and inconsistent formats (this is genuinely most of the job)
- **Analyse it** — find the pattern, the drop, the outlier, the trend
- **Communicate it** — a Power BI dashboard or a short deck that tells a manager what to do next

Notice that only two of those four steps are technical. The analysts who get promoted are the ones who are good at the fourth.

## Has AI made data analysts redundant?

This is the question every learner asks in 2026, so let's answer it directly: no — but it has changed which analysts get hired.

AI now handles a large share of the mechanical work that used to fill a junior analyst's week: writing a first-draft SQL query, cleaning a messy column, generating a standard chart, summarising a table. What AI cannot do is decide *which* question is worth asking, judge whether an answer is plausible given how the business actually works, or walk a non-technical manager through an uncomfortable finding.

So the roles under pressure are the pure report-generation jobs — someone who only refreshes the same dashboard every Monday. The roles growing are analysts who use AI to move faster and then apply judgement on top. Industry commentary through 2026 consistently lands on the same point, and the World Economic Forum still lists data and AI analyst roles among the fastest-growing globally through 2030.

**What this means for you practically:** learn the fundamentals properly *and* learn to work with AI tools. If you skip the fundamentals, you can't tell when the AI is confidently wrong — and that's the entire value you're being hired for.

## The skills employers actually test for

In interviews for entry-level analytics roles in India, the same four things come up again and again:

### 1. SQL — non-negotiable

SQL is the single most-requested skill in Indian data analyst job descriptions, and it's what you'll be tested on live. You need joins, aggregations, filtering, grouping and window function basics — enough to pull a business answer out of three related tables without help.

### 2. Excel — still everywhere

Pivot tables, lookups and clean formula logic. Plenty of real analysis in Indian SMEs and mid-size firms still happens in a spreadsheet, and interviewers use Excel to check whether you think clearly about data.

### 3. Power BI or Tableau — one, done well

Power BI dominates Indian enterprises; Tableau shows up more at global product companies and consulting firms. Learn one properly — data modelling, a working grasp of DAX, and dashboards designed for a reader rather than for showing off.

### 4. Python — not mandatory, but it separates candidates

Python with Pandas isn't required for every analyst role, but it's what lets you automate repetitive work and handle datasets Excel chokes on. It's also the bridge to data science later.

Underneath all four sits the skill nobody puts on a syllabus: **business sense**. Knowing that a 40% spike in signups on one day is probably a tracking bug, not a marketing win, is what makes an analyst trusted.

## Realistic salaries in India

Being straight about money: entry-level data analyst salaries in India in 2026 generally sit around **₹3.5–6 LPA**, with B.Tech/MCA backgrounds at the upper end of that band. Candidates who combine SQL, Python and Power BI *with a portfolio of real projects* consistently report offers in the **₹6–9 LPA** range, even from non-tier-1 colleges. Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Delhi NCR pay meaningfully above the national average; smaller cities pay less but cost far less to live in, and remote roles have narrowed the gap.

Experienced analysts moving into senior or lead positions cross ₹10–20 LPA. (These are market averages compiled from sources like AmbitionBox and Glassdoor — indicative, not a guarantee. Your offer depends on your portfolio, your interview and the employer.)

The pattern worth noticing: the salary jump doesn't come from the certificate. It comes from the portfolio.

## A realistic 6-month learning plan

This is the sequence we'd recommend to anyone starting from zero. It's deliberately ordered so that you can show something useful after every stage.

1. **Month 1 — Data foundations & Excel.** How data is structured, how to clean it, pivot tables, and your first small analysis end to end.
2. **Month 2 — SQL.** Queries, joins, aggregations, data modelling. Practise on real datasets until writing a query feels automatic.
3. **Month 3 — Power BI.** Connect data, model relationships, DAX basics, and build a dashboard someone else can actually read.
4. **Month 4 — Python for analysis.** Pandas for wrangling and cleaning; automating the boring parts of months 1–3.
5. **Month 5 — Statistics & business metrics.** Descriptive statistics, KPIs, funnels, cohorts — the vocabulary of business decisions.
6. **Month 6 — AI-assisted analytics & portfolio.** Using AI to accelerate cleaning, querying and insight generation, plus packaging 3–4 projects into a portfolio you can defend in an interview.

If you're a **fresh graduate**, do this in a structured, full-time weekday rhythm and treat the portfolio as your substitute for work experience — it's the thing that gets you shortlisted.

If you're a **working professional**, you have an advantage most freshers don't: domain knowledge. An accounts executive who learns SQL and Power BI becomes a finance analyst faster than a generic fresher ever will. Study on weekends or evenings, and build your portfolio projects around problems from your own industry — that's the shortest path to a switch or a raise without pausing your income. ## Common mistakes that cost people the job

- **Collecting certificates instead of projects.** Nobody has ever been hired for a certificate alone. Interviewers ask you to explain a project.
- **Learning tools without context.** Knowing DAX syntax is not the same as knowing what to measure.
- **Skipping SQL for something shinier.** It's the most-tested skill; there's no route around it.
- **Building dashboards nobody can read.** Twelve charts on one screen is not analysis — it's decoration.
- **Not practising the explanation.** Rehearse walking through a finding out loud, in plain language, in two minutes.
